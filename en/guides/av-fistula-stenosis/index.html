AV fistula stenosis: signs, diagnosis and when to refer

Stenosis is the narrowing of a segment of the vascular access. It is the most common reason an arteriovenous fistula stops working well, and the step before thrombosis. This guide explains where it usually develops in each type of fistula, how to suspect it on exam and during dialysis, how it is confirmed and what happens next, including the nursing role at each step.

For professionals · Updated November 10, 2026 · 9-minute read

Key points

  • It is suspected chairside: a changing thrill or bruit, a vein that does not collapse when the arm is raised, cannulation that keeps getting harder, abnormal pressures, a blood pump speed that cannot be reached, or bleeding after needle removal that lasts more than 20 minutes [1,2].
  • Physical exam is reliable. Against angiography, it detected outflow stenosis with 92% sensitivity and 86% specificity, and inflow stenosis with 85% and 71% [4].
  • It has typical sites: near the anastomosis in radiocephalic fistulas, the cephalic arch in brachiocephalic fistulas, the venous anastomosis in grafts and the central veins after catheters [5,6].
  • Monitoring or surveillance. KDOQI 2019 puts clinical monitoring first and finds insufficient evidence for routine flow or imaging surveillance [2]. The Spanish GEMAV guideline is more supportive of access flow (Qa) surveillance [3].
  • Once a significant stenosis is confirmed, GEMAV recommends treating it promptly, with angioplasty or surgery, because of its high risk of thrombosis [1].

What it is and why it matters

The wall of the arterialized vein responds to high flow and repeated cannulation by thickening its inner layer (intimal hyperplasia). Where that thickening narrows the lumen, flow speeds up and becomes turbulent, and pressure rises upstream. The consequences follow one another: less flow available for dialysis, recirculation, inadequate dialysis, harder cannulation and, eventually, thrombosis, which can mean a catheter or a new access. Catching it in time is how the fistula is saved (if it has already clotted, see clotted AV fistula).

Where it develops

Access typeTypical site
Radiocephalic fistulaNear the anastomosis (juxta-anastomotic), in the first centimeters of the vein. It is also the leading reason a fistula fails to mature.
Brachiocephalic fistulaThe cephalic arch, where the vein joins the axillary vein near the shoulder [6].
Transposed brachiobasilic fistulaThe point where the transposed vein swings from its original bed into the new tunnel.
GraftThe venous anastomosis, from intimal hyperplasia [6].
Any accessThe central veins (subclavian, brachiocephalic), especially after catheters or pacemakers.

In a series of 278 patients with angiographically proven stenosis, almost half (46%) had it in the segment mobilized at surgery (the swing segment). Within it, the juxta-anastomotic site was the most common (63%), followed by the cephalic arch (19%) [5].

Warning signs

WhenWhat may point to a stenosis
Exam before needlingA thrill that is no longer continuous, a high-pitched or systolic-only bruit, a water-hammer pulse, a vein that does not collapse when the arm is raised, tense segments or, conversely, a flat fistula that does not fill [1,4].
During cannulationCannulation that keeps getting harder, poor flashback, clots on aspiration [7].
During dialysisThe prescribed blood pump speed cannot be reached, very negative arterial pre-pump pressure, high venous pressure, recirculation or a falling dialysis dose.
At needle removalBleeding lasting more than 20 minutes without excess anticoagulation [1].
In the armSwelling of the arm, hand or face, and collateral veins on the chest: suspect central stenosis.

A single isolated sign may mean nothing. What matters is change from that patient's usual pattern, so record findings at every session and compare.

Physical exam: inflow or outflow

Done well, the exam localizes most stenoses. In a study of 142 patients with a dysfunctional fistula, the exam agreed with angiography in 89% of outflow stenoses and 80% of inflow stenoses [4].

FindingInflow stenosis (artery, anastomosis)Outflow stenosis (draining vein)
Pulse in the veinWeak, flat fistulaHyperpulsatile upstream of the stenosis
ThrillReducedDiscontinuous, systolic only; strong at the narrowing
Arm elevationThe vein collapses excessivelyThe vein below the stenosis does not collapse
Pulse augmentation (occluding the vein further up)The pulse barely increasesAlready augmented at baseline

These are the signs used to localize stenosis on exam [1,4]. The maneuvers, step by step, are in the guide to the AV fistula physical exam.

Monitoring and surveillance

Guidelines distinguish two things:

  • Monitoring: the physical exam and the data from every dialysis session. Nurses do it at every cannulation.
  • Surveillance: periodic dedicated tests, such as access flow (Qa) measurement by dilution or Doppler, or ultrasound imaging.

KDOQI 2019 puts clinical monitoring first and considers the evidence insufficient to recommend routine fistula surveillance with flow, pressure or imaging on top of it [2]. The Spanish GEMAV guideline is more supportive of Qa-based surveillance programs and uses Doppler ultrasound as the confirmatory test when a stenosis is suspected [3]. In practice both agree on the essentials: a systematic exam at every session and action when something changes.

How it is confirmed

  • Doppler ultrasound: the first imaging test in experienced hands [1]. GEMAV considers a stenosis significant when it reduces the lumen by more than 50% and doubles the peak systolic velocity compared with the segment before it, plus at least one additional criterion: a residual lumen under 2 mm or a low or falling access flow. The full criteria and how to measure Qa are in the guide to AV fistula ultrasound.
  • Fistulography: reserved for inconclusive scans, and it allows treatment in the same session [1]. In the DICAF study of difficult-to-cannulate fistulas, it had a higher diagnostic yield than Doppler [7].

Treatment

The vascular access team decides. GEMAV recommends treating a significant stenosis promptly, with angioplasty or surgery, because of its risk of thrombosis [1]:

  • Percutaneous angioplasty: the most widely used option. It can be repeated if the stenosis recurs.
  • Surgery: for example, a new, more proximal anastomosis for a juxta-anastomotic stenosis, depending on the site and the center's experience.
  • Stent graft: considered for stenoses that recur after angioplasty, in some locations [2].

After treatment, monitoring continues. A treated stenosis can come back, and the signs are the same.

The nursing role

  • Examine before every cannulation and compare with what was recorded.
  • Avoid cannulating over the stenosis or just downstream of it, where flow is turbulent and the wall is different.
  • Record pressures, the blood pump speed achieved, bleeding times and cannulation difficulties.
  • Report changes early to the vascular access team. Treating a stenosis before the fistula clots is the best way to save the access.

Practice it in the simulator. In the fistula with a juxta-anastomotic stenosis, find the narrowing and the aliasing with color Doppler, measure the peak systolic velocity at the stenosis and 2 cm before it, calculate the ratio and measure Qa in the brachial artery. Then, in Cannulation mode, choose a site away from the stenosis.

Frequently asked questions

What is AV fistula stenosis?

A narrowing of a segment of the access, almost always from thickening of the vein's inner layer. It reduces flow, makes dialysis and cannulation harder, and raises the risk of thrombosis.

What are the signs of a stenosed fistula?

Changes in the thrill or bruit (discontinuous, high-pitched), a vein that does not collapse when the arm is raised, harder cannulation, abnormal machine pressures, a blood pump speed that cannot be reached, prolonged bleeding after needle removal, or a swollen arm if the stenosis is central.

How is fistula stenosis diagnosed?

First with the physical exam, which is fairly reliable. It is confirmed with Doppler ultrasound: a lumen reduction of more than 50% and higher velocity, plus additional criteria such as a residual lumen under 2 mm or low flow. Fistulography is reserved for inconclusive cases.

What is the difference between inflow and outflow stenosis?

Inflow stenosis is in the artery or the anastomosis: the fistula is flat, with a weak pulse and a weak thrill. Outflow stenosis is in the draining vein: the vein becomes hyperpulsatile before the narrowing and does not collapse when the arm is raised.

How is AV fistula stenosis treated?

Usually with percutaneous angioplasty, or with surgery depending on the site. A stent graft may be considered for recurrent stenosis. The Spanish guideline recommends treating a significant stenosis promptly because of its risk of thrombosis.

Can dialysis continue with a stenosis?

Often yes, while treatment is scheduled, but with close monitoring: the risk of thrombosis is high. How to dialyze in the meantime is the nephrology team's decision.
