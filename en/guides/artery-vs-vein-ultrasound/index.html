Artery vs vein on ultrasound: how to tell them apart

Mistaking an artery for a vein is the error behind most complications of ultrasound-guided needling: a peripheral IV, a central line or a fistula cannulation. This guide covers six ways to tell them apart with the probe, explains why Doppler color does not tell you which is which, and describes the situations that fool even experienced operators.

For professionals · Updated November 11, 2026 · 8-minute read

Key points

  • Compressibility: a vein flattens with light probe pressure; an artery stays round. It is the quickest test [1].
  • Pulsation and wall: an artery pulsates and has a thicker wall; a vein is more oval, thin-walled and sometimes shows valves.
  • Pulsed-wave Doppler: an arm artery at rest gives a pulsatile, usually triphasic waveform; a vein gives continuous flow that varies with respiration [2].
  • Color does not tell you: red and blue show whether blood moves toward or away from the probe, not whether it is an artery or a vein. And with the probe perpendicular to the vessel there may be no color at all.
  • Use several signs. In a fistula, in hypotension or with a thrombus, any single one can mislead.

1. Compressibility: the quickest test

In short axis, with the vessel centered, increase probe pressure gradually. The vein flattens until it disappears with pressure that barely dents the skin; the artery stays round and pulsatile [1]. Do the test before you needle, not after. And on a superficial vein, use plenty of gel and rest your hand: if you press without meaning to while needling, the vein vanishes from the screen.

2. Pulsation, shape and wall

An artery expands with every beat; its wall, with intima and media, looks thicker and brighter. A vein is more oval and thin-walled, and in long axis its valves may be visible. Beware of pulsation: paired veins lying against an artery "pulsate" because the artery moves them, and central veins have their own cardiac pulsatility. On its own, it is not enough.

3. Color Doppler: what it tells you and what it does not

Color Doppler assigns a color according to whether blood moves toward or away from the probe, not whether it is an artery or a vein. With the usual map, red is toward the probe and blue is away, but the map can be inverted and an artery can look blue. Two practical points:

  • With the probe perpendicular to the vessel there is no color: the Doppler effect depends on the angle, and at 90° it disappears. Tilt the probe or steer the color box.
  • What helps is the pattern: in an artery, color appears and brightens with each beat; in a vein, filling is continuous, increases with a distal squeeze and changes with breathing.

4. Pulsed-wave Doppler: the definitive test

Place the sample volume inside the vessel. An arm artery at rest gives a pulsatile, usually triphasic, high-resistance waveform: a sharp systolic peak, a brief reversal and a small forward wave in late diastole. A vein gives continuous, low-velocity flow that varies with respiration and increases if you squeeze the arm below [2]. If you are in doubt before a high-risk puncture, pulsed-wave Doppler settles it.

5. Tourniquet and maneuvers

With a tourniquet above, the vein distends and the artery does not change. Squeezing the forearm or hand below the probe briefly increases venous flow (distal augmentation), which does not happen in an artery.

6. Anatomy

In the forearm, the radial and ulnar arteries are each flanked by two paired veins. The superficial veins used for IVs and fistulas (cephalic, basilic, median) run alone in the subcutaneous tissue, more superficial and with no artery alongside. At the elbow and in the upper arm, the brachial artery runs with its veins and next to the median nerve: locate them before needling the basilic vein or an upper-arm fistula.

Why it matters for every puncture

  • Ultrasound-guided peripheral IV: at the elbow and in the upper arm, accidental arterial puncture is a real risk. Check compressibility before needling (see ultrasound-guided peripheral IV).
  • Fistula cannulation: in brachiocephalic and transposed brachiobasilic fistulas, the brachial artery lies medial to or beneath the vein (see types of AV fistula).
  • Vein mapping: measuring the radial artery and not a paired vein changes the surgical decision (see vein mapping).
  • Central venous catheters: the US Society of Hospital Medicine recommends real-time ultrasound for central venous access, in large part to reduce complications such as arterial puncture [3].

Practice it in the simulator. In the guided lesson, on the arm without a fistula, compress over the radial artery and watch the two paired veins collapse while the artery stays round and pulsatile. Turn on color Doppler: with the probe perpendicular there is no color; tilt it. Finally, use pulsed-wave Doppler to compare the artery's triphasic waveform with the continuous flow of a superficial vein. Then repeat on the mature fistula and see how the radial artery waveform changes.

Frequently asked questions

How do you tell an artery from a vein on ultrasound?

By compressibility (the vein flattens with little pressure), pulsation, shape and wall, and above all by pulsed-wave Doppler: a pulsatile waveform in the artery and continuous flow that varies with breathing in the vein. Use several signs together.

Is red the artery and blue the vein?

No. Doppler color shows whether blood moves toward or away from the probe. An artery can look blue and a vein red depending on probe orientation and the color map.

Why is there no color on Doppler?

Often because the probe is perpendicular to the vessel: at 90° there is no Doppler effect. Tilt the probe or steer the color box. Velocity scale and color gain also matter.

Is a vein that does not compress an artery?

Not necessarily: it may be a thrombosed vein. The difference is that it does not pulsate and has no Doppler flow. It should not be needled.

Why does the fistula vein pulsate?

Because it receives blood straight from the artery. The arterialized vein pulsates, has fast, turbulent flow and compresses less easily than a normal vein. To avoid confusion, follow it from the anastomosis.
