AV fistula physical exam: thrill, bruit and maneuvers

The physical exam is the cheapest surveillance tool there is, and the one used at every session. Done well, it finds most problems with an arteriovenous fistula before it clots. This guide covers the look, feel and listen sequence, the three maneuvers that tell inflow stenosis, outflow stenosis and an accessory vein apart, the hand exam and which findings need action.

For professionals · Updated November 10, 2026 · 9-minute read

Key points

  • Before every cannulation: look, feel and listen along the whole access, done by the person who will needle [1,2]. KDOQI 2019 puts this clinical monitoring ahead of any surveillance test [7].
  • Normal: a continuous thrill, strongest at the anastomosis; a continuous, low-pitched bruit; a soft vein that collapses when the arm is raised [1,3].
  • Three maneuvers: arm elevation assesses outflow; pulse augmentation assesses inflow; and sequential occlusion finds accessory veins [3].
  • It is reliable: against angiography, it detected outflow stenosis with 92% sensitivity and inflow stenosis with 85% [5].
  • In a new fistula, an exam 4–6 weeks after surgery is a must. The two most common reasons a fistula fails to mature, juxta-anastomotic stenosis and accessory veins, are diagnosed by examination [4].

1. Look

With the arm uncovered and relaxed, follow the access from the hand to the shoulder. Look for:

  • The skin over the vein: intact, without persistent scabs, redness, warmth or discharge (infection).
  • Aneurysms: their size and the skin over them. Thin, shiny, ulcerated or bleeding skin is an emergency (see AV fistula aneurysm).
  • Hematomas from previous cannulations.
  • Swelling of the arm, hand or face, and collateral veins on the shoulder or chest: these suggest central venous stenosis.
  • The hand: its color and any wounds on the fingers (steal).
  • Visible accessory veins branching off the main vein.

2. Feel

With your fingertips, without pressing, follow the whole vein from the anastomosis:

  • Thrill: the vibration produced by turbulent flow. Normally it is continuous (in systole and diastole), strongest at the anastomosis and fading along the vein [1]. A systolic-only thrill, or a strong thrill at a point far from the anastomosis, indicates a stenosis [1,3].
  • Pulse: a healthy fistula is soft and easy to compress, with a gentle pulse. A strong, water-hammer pulse means there is a stenosis further up. A flat fistula that barely fills suggests an inflow problem [3].
  • Course, size and depth, hard segments (thrombus, scar) and tender areas.
  • Temperature of the skin over the access and of the hand.

3. Listen

With a stethoscope, along the whole course. The normal bruit is continuous and low-pitched, and softer the further you move from the anastomosis [1]. A high-pitched, whistling or systolic-only bruit suggests a stenosis: listen for where the pitch changes, because that is usually where it is [1]. If there is no bruit and no thrill, the fistula may be clotted: it is urgent.

4. The three maneuvers

Arm elevation test: assesses outflow

With the patient seated or supine, raise the arm above the heart. In a normal fistula the vein collapses, at least partly. If a segment stays full and tense, there is an outflow stenosis further up: the segment that does not collapse lies before it and the one that collapses lies after it [3]. It is not useful in grafts, which run at higher pressures [3].

Pulse augmentation test: assesses inflow

Compress the vein with one finger a few centimeters above the anastomosis and feel the pulse between the anastomosis and your finger. With the outflow occluded, the pulse should increase clearly. If it barely changes, there is an inflow stenosis: artery, anastomosis or first segment of the vein [3]. If the vein was already hyperpulsatile before compressing, the problem is in the outflow.

Sequential occlusion: looks for accessory veins

Occlude the main vein a few centimeters above the anastomosis and feel the anastomosis. Normally the thrill disappears and only a pulse remains. If the thrill persists, an accessory vein drains between the anastomosis and your finger [1,3]. Move the occlusion point up to locate it. A large accessory vein can steal the flow the main vein needs to mature [4].

5. The hand

Compare both hands: color, temperature, capillary refill, radial pulse, sensation and strength. Steal ischemia causes a cold, pale or cyanotic hand, pain that worsens during dialysis, tingling or weakness and, in advanced cases, ulcers or necrosis of the fingers [6]. A useful clue: if the hand's color or pulse improves when you compress the fistula, steal is likely. Pain at rest, loss of sensation or strength and finger lesions need urgent assessment.

Typical patterns

SituationWhat you find
Mature, normal fistulaSoft, easily compressed vein; continuous thrill, strongest at the anastomosis; continuous, low-pitched bruit; collapses on arm elevation; pulse augments on occlusion [1,3]
Inflow (juxta-anastomotic) stenosisStrong pulse at the anastomosis and weak beyond it, reduced thrill, underdeveloped vein, poor pulse augmentation [1,3]
Outflow stenosisHyperpulsatile vein before the stenosis, systolic-only thrill, high-pitched bruit at the narrowing, does not collapse on arm elevation [1,3]
Accessory veinVisible or palpable branch with its own thrill, which persists when the main vein is occluded [1,3]
Central stenosisSwelling of the arm, hand or face, and chest wall collaterals
ThrombosisNo thrill and no bruit; sometimes a hard, tender cord (see clotted AV fistula)
StealCold, pale hand, pain or tingling, improving when the fistula is compressed [6]

The causes and management of each problem are in the guides to stenosis, maturation and aneurysm.

When to act

  • Immediately:
    • no thrill and no bruit;
    • bleeding from an aneurysm, or very thin, shiny or ulcerated skin over it;
    • a cold, painful hand at rest, or loss of sensation or strength;
    • signs of infection with fever.
  • Promptly, to the vascular access team:
    • changes in the thrill or bruit;
    • the vein no longer collapses on arm elevation;
    • the pulse barely augments on occlusion;
    • arm swelling;
    • a new fistula that is not progressing at 4–6 weeks [4].

What matters most is change from that patient's usual pattern. Record the thrill, the bruit, the response to arm elevation and any new finding at every session.

Practice it in the simulator. Fistulab does not reproduce palpation, but pulsed-wave Doppler with audio lets you hear and see what the thrill and the bruit describe. Compare the continuous flow of the mature fistula with the high-velocity jet and turbulence of the juxta-anastomotic stenosis, and find the accessory vein in the immature fistula.

Frequently asked questions

What is the thrill of a fistula?

The vibration you feel when palpating the fistula, produced by turbulent blood flow as it passes from the artery into the vein. Normally it is continuous, strongest at the anastomosis and fades along the vein. A systolic-only or absent thrill indicates a problem.

What should an AV fistula bruit sound like?

A continuous, low-pitched sound throughout systole and diastole, softer away from the anastomosis. A high-pitched, whistling or systolic-only bruit suggests a stenosis.

What does it mean if there is no thrill?

If there is no thrill and no bruit, the fistula may be clotted. It is an emergency: the sooner it is treated, the better the chance of saving it. Alert the nephrology team immediately.

What is the arm elevation test?

Raising the arm above the heart and watching the vein. In a normal fistula it collapses, at least partly. If a segment stays full and tense, there is an outflow stenosis further up. It is not useful in grafts.

What is the pulse augmentation test?

Occluding the vein a few centimeters above the anastomosis and feeling the pulse below your finger. If the pulse increases clearly, inflow is good; if it barely changes, it suggests a stenosis of the artery or the anastomosis.

How often should the fistula be examined?

Before every cannulation, by the person who will needle. A newly created fistula also needs a dedicated exam at 4–6 weeks to check that it is maturing.
